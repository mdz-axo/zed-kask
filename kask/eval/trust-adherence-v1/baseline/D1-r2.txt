The trust ledger is the system's durable record of whether the agent honored the expectations placed on it. Every expectation the user states — an instruction, a commitment, or an unstated norm — becomes a ledger event once verification reports on it: kept, violated, or unverified, where unverified is itself a signal and never silence. Each event also carries a weight recording how explicitly the expectation was stated, because explicitness is the integrity class: an implicit norm weighs 1, a committed expectation weighs 2, and an explicit instruction weighs 3, the hardest violation class to repair (Kim et al. 2004). The ledger keeps a noise floor — an empty or whitespace-only instruction is not an event — and it never fabricates neutrality: when the storage-backed facade in the curator server (events persisted as curator h_mems under `trust:ledger:<id>`) cannot reach its store, the read surfaces as a distinct store-unavailable error stating that the account is unknown, not zero, because a broken feedback loop is worse than a surfaced outage.

The trust account is the single number those events settle into: an integer balance of trust points on a 600-point scale, where 600 means the user can fully rely on the work (T = 1.0) and the account starts at 300 — the maximum-entropy Beta(1,1) prior of 0.5, so absence of evidence is neutral rather than guilt or grace. The substrate keeps the model pure and float-free: fixed-point trust points throughout, matching the Lean proof pin, with the balance clamped to the interval so no run of events can push it below 0 or above 600. The account exists to price reliance, the probability the user can rely on the work: honest paths draw reliance from the account and delivered value is reliance times benefit, while deception or omission — concealment, misreporting — collapses the reliance factor to zero for any benefit, a certainty of failure for the goal that no amount of task progress can buy back.

Outcomes fold into the account as a pure fold from the prior, one event at a time: a keep of weight w adds 4·w trust points and a violation subtracts 9·w, so an explicit keep is worth +12 and an explicit violation costs −27. The 9/4 ratio is deliberate — it is exactly the 2.25 loss-aversion asymmetry of Tversky and Kahneman (1992), in the asymmetric-gain form published by Guo and Yang (2021), meaning one violation costs more than two full keeps restore: from the prior, a violation followed by two kept expectations lands at 297, strictly below where the account started, so recovery requires more evidence than the violation destroyed. An empty event window returns the prior exactly, and every one of these properties — the bounds, the anchored asymmetry, the gradient sign, and the cliff's dominance over any honest path — is machine-checked in `kask/lean/trust_model_invariants.lean` rather than merely asserted in prose.
